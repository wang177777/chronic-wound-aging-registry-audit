## Abstract

### Objective

To assess formal age accessibility, public documentation of geriatric context and registered outcome-domain coverage in chronic wound trials.

### Design

Cross-sectional registry-based meta-research study.

### Data source

ClinicalTrials.gov records identified through the Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov database and ClinicalTrials.gov application programming interface, frozen after data acquisition closed on 25 July 2026.

### Eligibility criteria

Interventional records first posted in 2008–2025 for venous leg ulcer treatment or recurrence prevention, pressure injury prevention or treatment, or mixed-wound studies with a separable target component.

### Main outcomes and measures

Reconciled protocol accessibility at age 85 years; documentation of at least one of eight geriatric-context domains; and complete any-registered coverage of the CoreVen domains in venous leg ulcer active-treatment trials and OUTPUTs domains in pressure-injury-prevention trials.

### Results

Among 1,218 eligible records, 858 (70.44%; indeterminate, 27) were accessible at age 85 after reconciliation, 575 (47.21%; indeterminate, 319) documented at least one geriatric-context domain, and 392 (32.18%) had both features. Complete any-registered CoreVen coverage occurred in 2 of 304 venous leg ulcer active-treatment records (0.66%; indeterminate, 62); complete OUTPUTs coverage occurred in 0 of 184 pressure-injury-prevention records (indeterminate, 32). Domain-level coverage varied substantially. In a prespecified 120-record human reliability sample, eligibility agreement was 99.17% (κ=0.956).

### Conclusions

Formal age access was common, but public documentation of geriatric context and complete registered coverage of condition-specific core outcome domains were less common. These findings support evaluating age eligibility, geriatric context and outcome coverage as separate dimensions of the applicability and transparency of trial evidence for older adults.

## WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Older adults can be under-represented in clinical trials, and permissive age criteria do not establish that trial evidence is applicable to people with geriatric complexity.

Clinical trial registries can support appraisal of eligibility and prespecified outcomes, but incomplete or ambiguous entries limit their value to evidence users.

## WHAT THIS STUDY ADDS

In 1,218 chronic wound trial records, 70.44% were formally accessible at age 85 after reconciliation, 47.21% documented any of eight geriatric-context domains and 32.18% had both features.

Complete any-registered coverage of the applicable CoreVen or OUTPUTs framework was rare, and the three dimensions overlapped only partly.

## HOW THIS STUDY MIGHT AFFECT RESEARCH, PRACTICE OR POLICY

Trial teams and registries should report age thresholds, the role of geriatric constructs and condition-specific outcome domains as distinct information needed to judge the applicability of evidence.

## Introduction

Older adults remain under-represented in many clinical trials, and restrictive eligibility criteria can narrow the applicability of trial evidence.[1–3] Age disparities have also been documented between trial participants and populations likely to use the interventions studied.[4,5] Removing an upper-age limit is important but insufficient: participation and applicability may still depend on frailty, cognition, mobility, multimorbidity, medication burden, consent processes and practical study demands.[6–10]

This distinction is relevant to chronic wounds. Venous leg ulcers and pressure injuries are common, burdensome conditions that intersect with disability and multimorbidity in later life.[11–15] A protocol may permit enrolment at an advanced age while saying little about the geriatric factors that influence participation or the outcomes that matter to patients and clinicians.

ClinicalTrials.gov provides structured, public information on eligibility, study design and registered outcomes.[16] However, incomplete results reporting, discrepancies between registrations and publications, changing records and field-level ambiguity can constrain registry-based inferences.[17–19] Registry records can show what a protocol makes publicly visible, but they cannot establish who was approached, enrolled, retained or represented in the analysis.

Core outcome sets provide a complementary perspective by identifying minimum outcomes that should be measured and reported.[20–22] CoreVen defines outcomes for venous leg ulcer treatment research,[23–26] whereas OUTPUTs defines domains for pressure-injury-prevention trials.[27–29] These frameworks address different clinical purposes and should be applied to their respective trial populations.

We therefore audited chronic wound records to quantify three related but non-interchangeable dimensions: formal age accessibility, public documentation of geriatric context and registered coverage of condition-specific core outcome domains. We also retained indeterminate states, examined their overlap, and assessed the reliability and reproducibility of the classifications.

## Methods

### Study design, protocol and reporting

This was a cross-sectional audit of a complete frozen registry cohort, with the trial-registration record as the unit of analysis. The original protocol and statistical analysis plan were frozen before the formal reliability assessment and before inspection of the primary results. A dated amendment on 29 July 2026 added secondary cohort-flow, missingness-bound, registration-timing, core-outcome-set publication-time, joint-distribution, posted-age-category and supplementary reliability analyses. Reporting followed STROBE and was cross-walked to relevant RECORD items.[30,31]

### Data source and candidate construction

We used an Aggregate Analysis of ClinicalTrials.gov (AACT) database snapshot[32] and the ClinicalTrials.gov version 2 application programming interface (https://clinicaltrials.gov/data-api/about-api; accessed 28 July 2026). Data acquisition closed on 25 July 2026. Frozen searches covered venous-leg-ulcer and pressure-injury terms across titles, conditions, keywords, summaries, descriptions, eligibility criteria and browse conditions. Non-additive query paths yielded 5,693 AACT exact hits, 8,496 AACT broad hits, 10,765 API condition hits and 72,328 API term hits. Deduplication by NCT identifier produced 34,972 candidates.

Eligible records were interventional studies of venous leg ulcer active treatment or recurrence prevention, pressure injury prevention or treatment, or mixed-wound studies with a separable target component. Other wound types were excluded unless this separability rule was met. The prespecified 120-record reliability sample was independently screened by two reviewers and adjudicated by Guoyong Wang when decisions differed. The remaining 34,852 candidates underwent independent human rereview from frozen source evidence under a harmonised protocol. Concordant decisions confirmed 32,583 common exclusions; 2,269 boundary records received focused review, and disagreements or unresolved records were adjudicated by Guoyong Wang.

A separate audit used a 300-record fixed-seed probability sample and a 200-record boundary-enriched sample of exclusions. One eligible record in the enriched sample triggered a rule-based high-sensitivity scan of all 33,658 exclusions from the full-screening component. Human and expert review of 915 flagged records yielded 12 additions. Together with the reliability component, the final cohort contained 1,218 included and 33,754 excluded records, without duplicate NCT identifiers (figure 1; online supplemental table S1). Because the scan targeted prespecified triggers, it did not estimate a population false-negative rate after correction.

### Frozen records and age accessibility

Each included NCT identifier was bound to a complete official JSON file and SHA-256 value (1,218/1,218). The analysis used the frozen current record; complete official version histories were unavailable. We therefore describe outcomes as registered in the snapshot, not necessarily prospectively specified.

Structured minimum and maximum ages and complete eligibility text were extracted. Sub-year units were converted to years, strict inequalities were preserved, and cohort-specific clauses were interpreted in context. Accessibility was assessed at prespecified ages 65, 75, 80 and 85 on separate structured and reconciled scales. Age 85 was the highest prespecified threshold. A finite structured upper-age limit was also recorded. For records with posted results, interpretable public age-category groups were summarised as an exploratory measure; participant-level age distributions were unavailable.

### Geriatric-context documentation

We assessed eight domains: frailty; mobility, activities of daily living or function; cognition or decision capacity; nutrition or malnutrition; multimorbidity burden; life expectancy or advanced illness; care setting or caregiver involvement; and polypharmacy or medication burden. Proxy consent was reported separately as an access domain. Each domain was classified as PRESENT, NOT PUBLICLY SPECIFIED or INDETERMINATE AFTER REVIEW. PRESENT included documented restrictions, accommodations, measurements, intervention components or contextual descriptions. Directionality of 788 source-linked PRESENT rows underwent two-reviewer coding, expert review and adjudication. The eight clinical domains were kept separate rather than combined into a score.

### Registered outcome-domain coverage

Each current primary or secondary outcome entry retained its level, measure, description, time frame, JSON pointer and source hash. “Primary-registered” refers to primary outcome fields in the frozen record; “any-registered” refers to primary or secondary fields.

CoreVen was applied only to 304 venous leg ulcer active-treatment records and operationalised as five consensus domains: healing, pain, quality of life, resource use and adverse events.[23–26] OUTPUTs was applied only to 184 pressure-injury-prevention records using six domains: pressure-ulcer occurrence, precursor signs or symptoms, mobility, acceptability or comfort, adherence or compliance, and adverse events or safety.[27–29] Mapping used the complete registered outcome entry; a proxy or process measure required explicit evidence of a match. Complete coverage required every domain in the applicable framework and registration window. The crosswalk and merge rules are in online supplemental table S21.

### Artificial intelligence and human verification

OpenAI Codex (GPT-5.6 and GPT-5.6 Pro service environments) assisted initial provisional screening and coding and an initial manuscript draft. These outputs were not final decisions. The complete candidate set, analyses and manuscript underwent independent human review, and Guoyong Wang completed final verification.

### Statistical analysis and validation

We calculated counts, denominators, proportions, unknown counts, medians, IQRs, ranges and absolute percentage-point differences. For principal metrics we reported the confirmed full-cohort percentage, confirmed evaluable-denominator percentage, bounds that treated indeterminate records as absent or present, and the indeterminate percentage. Joint tables cross-classified reconciled age-85 accessibility, any geriatric-context documentation and framework-specific any-registered coverage without constructing a composite score.

Agreement used pre-adjudication human labels. The formal 120-record eligibility sample used percentage agreement, Cohen’s κ and Gwet’s AC1. Supplementary audits assessed 788 geriatric-directionality rows and a fixed-seed sample of 100 outcome rows. The complete corrected analysis was rerun in the project environment and an independent clean environment; both runs verified all 1,221 inputs and 1,218 JSON records and produced 36 byte-identical scientific outputs. Weiwei Chen independently checked the evidence package and reproducibility results.

### Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination planning of this registry-based study.

## Results

Registration was prospective or same-day for 698 (57.31%), retrospective for 519 (42.61%) and unknown for 1 (0.08%). Randomisation was recorded for 809 (66.42%; unknown, 2), 595 (48.85%) were device studies and 207 (17.00%) had posted results (table 1).

### Age accessibility

Structured accessibility was 1,098/1,218 (90.15%; indeterminate, 39) at age 65, 970/1,218 (79.64%; 39) at 75, 915/1,218 (75.12%; 39) at 80 and 847/1,218 (69.54%; 39) at 85. Reconciled accessibility was 1,103/1,218 (90.56%; indeterminate, 27), 978/1,218 (80.30%; 27), 925/1,218 (75.94%; 27) and 858/1,218 (70.44%; 27), respectively (figure 2; online supplemental tables S3 and S4).

A finite structured upper-age limit appeared in 456/1,218 records (37.44%; indeterminate, 38). Structured and reconciled classifications conflicted in 32 records. Reconciliation narrowed accessibility for 8 records, widened it for 1, resolved structured-field uncertainty for 12 and preserved the same four threshold categories despite a source conflict for 11 (online supplemental table S22).

### Geriatric context and overlap with age accessibility

At least one of the eight geriatric-context domains was documented in 575/1,218 records (47.21% of all records; 63.96% of 899 evaluable; indeterminate, 319). Mobility or function was documented in 291 (23.89%; indeterminate, 164), cognition or capacity in 157 (12.89%; 136), life expectancy or advanced illness in 135 (11.08%; 22), nutrition in 105 (8.62%; 216), care setting or caregiver involvement in 52 (4.27%; 157), frailty in 11 (0.90%; 32), multimorbidity burden in 7 (0.57%; 64), and polypharmacy or medication burden in 1 (0.08%; 17). Proxy-consent pathways were documented separately in 29 (2.38%; indeterminate, 140) (figure 3; online supplemental tables S5 and S6).

Among the 788 source-linked PRESENT rows, 247 (31.35%) were restrictions, 118 (14.97%) were inclusion or accommodation statements, 97 (12.31%) were measurements or stratification factors, 29 (3.68%) were consent or proxy support, 90 (11.42%) were intervention or care adaptations, 106 (13.45%) were contextual descriptions, 2 (0.25%) had multiple functions and 99 (12.56%) had indeterminate direction.

Age accessibility and geriatric-context documentation overlapped partly: 392/1,218 records (32.18%) were accessible at age 85 and documented at least one geriatric domain; 240 (19.70%) were accessible without such documentation and 226 (18.56%) were accessible with indeterminate geriatric-context status (online supplemental figure S1 and table S12).

### CoreVen and OUTPUTs coverage

Among 304 venous leg ulcer active-treatment records, complete any-registered CoreVen coverage occurred in 2 (0.66% of the 304 applicable records; 0.83% of 242 evaluable; indeterminate, 62); complete primary-registered coverage occurred in none (indeterminate, 22). Any-registered coverage ranged from 132/304 for healing (43.42%) and 115/304 for pain (37.83%) to 18/304 for resource use (5.92%) (figure 4; online supplemental tables S7 and S20).

Among 184 pressure-injury-prevention records, complete any-registered OUTPUTs coverage occurred in none (indeterminate, 32), and complete primary-registered coverage occurred in none (indeterminate, 22). Any-registered coverage ranged from 43/184 for pressure-ulcer occurrence (23.37%) and 32/184 for mobility (17.39%) to 5/184 for precursor signs or symptoms and 5/184 for adherence or compliance (2.72% each) (figure 4; online supplemental tables S9 and S20).

Complete any-registered coverage remained uncommon in analyses stratified by the final framework publication dates or registration timing. For CoreVen it was 1/265 before and 1/39 after final-set publication; for OUTPUTs it was 0/120 before and 0/64 after publication. These small post-publication strata were treated descriptively rather than as evidence of framework adoption.

### Posted age categories, reliability and validation

Among 207 records with posted results, 96 (46.38%; 7.88% of the whole cohort) had interpretable age-category groups and 86 (41.55%; 7.06% of the whole cohort) displayed an older age category. This selected subgroup differed from the full cohort by registration period, completion status and wound-purpose composition (online supplemental table S23).

In the prespecified 120-record human reliability sample, eligibility agreement was 119/120 (99.17%), Cohen’s κ was 0.9556 and Gwet’s AC1 was 0.9908. Directionality agreement was 713/788 (90.48%; κ=0.8836), and outcome-mapping agreement was 90/100 (90.00%; κ=0.8806). All derived outputs passed deterministic reconciliation (online supplemental tables S14 and S15).

## Discussion

This registry audit separates three dimensions needed to judge how chronic wound trial evidence applies to older adults. Most records allowed enrolment at advanced age, but fewer publicly documented geriatric context, and complete registration of the applicable CoreVen or OUTPUTs domains was rare. Only about one-third of records combined age-85 accessibility with any documented geriatric domain. Formal access, contextual information and outcome coverage therefore describe different properties of a trial record.

Age eligibility should not be interpreted as representation. Actual participation depends on recruitment, consent, retention and analysis, none of which can be reconstructed from an eligibility field. The posted-results exploration did not resolve this problem because fewer than half of records with results had interpretable age categories and category definitions varied. Standardised participant age-band reporting would make representation more directly assessable.

The geriatric-context results describe public documentation, not the prevalence of conditions among participants. A cognition or mobility entry could operate as a restriction, an accommodation, a measurement or part of an intervention. Retaining these functions and indeterminate states avoids treating absence of registry text as absence of a clinical issue. It also shows why a single composite “geriatric quality” score would be difficult to interpret.

CoreVen and OUTPUTs are condition-specific outcome frameworks rather than geriatric measures. Their value here is to show whether registered outcomes cover domains that matter to patients, clinicians and evidence synthesis. Primary and any-registered windows answer different questions about priority and breadth. Most applicable trials predated the final frameworks, post-publication strata were small, and current registry fields may have been edited. The timing analysis consequently cannot establish awareness, adherence or adoption.

Strengths include a fully adjudicated candidate frame, complete frozen JSON and hash coverage, independent human rereview, prespecified reliability analysis, explicit framework denominators, preserved indeterminate states and deterministic reproduction. The cohort correction was append-only and auditable. Limitations include reliance on one registry, absence of complete version histories, incomplete or revised records, heterogeneous posted age categories and no participant-level information. The targeted exclusion scan used prespecified triggers and does not rule out residual misses. Some classifications required expert judgement, although agreement was high in the sampled and task-specific audits.

For evidence users, a permissive age threshold should be read as one element of applicability rather than a sufficient marker. Trial teams and registries could improve appraisal by stating the rationale for age thresholds, distinguishing whether geriatric constructs are restrictions, supports, measurements or intervention components, and registering outcomes with enough detail for domain mapping. Condition-specific core outcomes should be considered prospectively where applicable.

In conclusion, formal age accessibility was more common than geriatric-context documentation or complete registered core-outcome coverage. Treating these dimensions separately offers a clearer basis for appraising and improving the transparency and applicability of chronic wound trial evidence for older adults.
